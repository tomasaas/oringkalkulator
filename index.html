Background

What matters, and why

An O-ring seals because it is squeezed, and because it has somewhere to go when it heats up. These are the numbers that decide whether it holds.

Squeeze

It is the squeeze that seals

The ring is fitted oversized and pressed flat between groove bottom and sealing face. While its contact pressure exceeds the pressure of the medium, nothing gets past.

  • Pressure adds to the contact pressure, so a slack seal leaks at low pressure, not at maximum.
  • Too little: leaks at start-up, in the cold, and once the rubber has taken a set.
  • Too much: friction, heat, faster ageing. Above ~30 % the life drops noticeably.
  • On thin cross sections 15 % is very little in mm, so the absolute squeeze is checked too.

Gland fill

Rubber cannot be compressed

Rubber can be shaped, not squashed. Pressed flat, its volume moves sideways, so the groove must be wider than the ring is thick. Fill the groove completely and you get hydraulic lock: several hundred bar in the groove, extruded rubber, burst grooves, flanges lifted apart.

The 15 % margin is not slack. It is reserved for:

  • Heat. Rubber expands 9–20× more than steel: 3–8 % by volume per 100 °C.
  • Chemical swell in the medium, often 2–10 %.
  • Tolerances on cross section and groove.

Below ~50 % fill the groove is so roomy the ring can roll — a known cause of spiral failure.

Stretch

Stretch steals squeeze

Volume is constant, so what gets longer gets thinner: 5 % stretch takes about 2 % off the cross section. This tool conserves the ring volume and uses the fitted cross section, so squeeze reads slightly lower than a plain (b − h) / b would give.

Stretch also leaves a permanent tensile strain, which accelerates ageing and ozone cracking, NBR especially. On rod seals the ring is compressed on the outside instead — too much and it buckles into waves and leaks along them.

Extrusion gap

The gap the ring gets pushed into

Under pressure the rubber is forced into the clearance and nibbled apart at the edge, a little every cycle. Gap × pressure is what matters, not the gap alone.

  • Allowable gap falls roughly in inverse proportion to pressure.
  • 90 Shore A takes several times the gap that 70 Shore A does.
  • Hot rubber is softer and extrudes more easily.
  • Eccentricity puts the whole clearance on one side — always use the full diametral value.

Remedies, in order: tighter fit, harder compound, then a back-up ring on the low-pressure side. A back-up ring needs a wider groove.

Temperature

Cold leaks, heat wears

Cold: the rubber contracts and stiffens, losing both squeeze and the ability to follow surface irregularities. The usable limit sits 8–15 °C above the glass transition. Hot: it grows faster than the metal around it, squeeze and gland fill rise, and every 10 °C roughly halves the service life.

Compression set

The ring never gets its shape back

Rubber held compressed never fully recovers. The lost fraction, the compression set, grows with temperature and time: 50 % set means half the original squeeze is gone. That is why seals leak after months in service rather than at commissioning, and why it shows at start-up after a standstill — pressure still holds a tired seal, an unloaded cold joint does not.

For hot, long-life joints: squeeze in the upper half of the range, and a low-set compound rather than more squeeze.

Surface and assembly

The groove ruins more rings than the maths

  • Sealing face: 0.1–0.4 µm Ra dynamic, up to 0.8–1.6 µm Ra static. Groove bottom and sides take 1.6–3.2 µm Ra.
  • Below ~0.05 µm Ra the lubricant film will not stay put and friction rises.
  • Groove corners radiused 0.1–0.3 mm; sharp corners cut the ring on pressure spikes.
  • Groove sides vertical, up to 5° taper. Steeper lets the ring escape.
  • Lead-in chamfer 15–20°; cover or remove threads, edges and cross holes in the assembly path.
  • Lubricate on assembly with a medium the ring tolerates. Dry assembly tears the surface.

One scratch across the sealing face is enough.

Static and dynamic

Motion changes every requirement

A static seal is a gasket: high squeeze costs little. A dynamic seal has to slide too, so squeeze is held at 10–20 % — friction, heat and wear climb fast with it. Failure modes to watch:

  • Spiral failure. The ring rolls instead of sliding and twists apart. Long stroke, low speed, wide groove, cross section over 3 mm.
  • Stick-slip at low speed, as friction drops once motion starts.
  • Dry running at start-up, when the film has been squeezed away.

Rotary motion follows separate rules and is not covered by this tool.

Tolerances

It is the extremes that leak

A groove that is perfect at nominal can be useless in production, so every combination of limits is calculated, at assembly and in service. Read the range, not the nominal number.

The ring's own tolerance usually dominates, and it is relatively worst on small rings: ±0.13 mm on a 3.7 mm ID is ±3.5 %, the same tolerance on a 100 mm ring only ±0.3 %. Squeeze and stretch can then land outside the range on ring tolerance alone, however accurately the groove is machined. Tighten the ring spec, go up a cross section, or accept the wider window deliberately.

Order of work

How the choices hang together

  1. Cross section first: thicker takes more wear, set and tolerance, but needs space and adds friction.
  2. Diameter so the stretch stays small. Piston: ring ID just under the groove root. Rod: ring OD just over it.
  3. Groove depth from the target squeeze.
  4. Groove width from the gland fill, with margin for heat and swell.
  5. Clearance against pressure and hardness; add a back-up ring if needed.
  6. Material against medium, temperature and pressure.

“Suggest dimensions” does steps 3–5. In Advanced mode, lock what the design already fixes and it is kept.

Assumptions, formulas and sources

Notation. a ring ID, b cross section, c bore, d piston or rod diameter, e groove root diameter, L groove width. An axial groove is given by its inner diameter c, radial width L, depth h and the flange gap s; its outer diameter is c + 2L.

Gland depth. Piston h = (c − e)/2, rod h = (e − d)/2; for an axial groove it is the groove depth itself.

Effective cross section. The ring volume V = π²·b²·(a + b)/4 is held constant during installation, so b' solves b'²·(D ± b') = b²·(a + b) at the groove root diameter D the ring rests against (+ inside fit, − outside fit). A geometric approximation of stretch thinning the cross section, not a material model.

Squeeze. 100 · (b' − h) / b', with b' − h checked against 0.13 mm static and 0.10 mm dynamic. Gland fill is the ring volume over the annular groove volume — π·(c² − e²)·L/4 piston, π·(e² − d²)·L/4 rod, π·((c + 2L)² − c²)·h/4 axial — which, unlike a plain area ratio, accounts for ring and groove sitting at different mean diameters. Stretch is 100·(D/a − 1) inside, and outside a reduction of the free OD, 100·(1 − D/(a + 2b)).

Tolerances. All 64 combinations of extremes for the ring and the four groove dimensions, at assembly (20 °C, no swell) and at the stated operating point. The ranges shown are the extremes of that.

Temperature and swell. Linear expansion, α = 112 / 120 / 162 / 160 / 250 / 150 µm/(m·K) for NBR / HNBR / FKM / EPDM / VMQ / FFKM, against the value selected for the housing: 11–23 for the metals, 47–140 for unfilled plastics. Chemical volume change gives a length factor ∛(1 + ΔV/100). Coefficients are approximate and vary between compounds and grades.

Extrusion limit. Indicative curve g = K / p^n with (K, n) = (2.06, 1.00), (2.10, 0.80) and (2.21, 0.63) for 70, 80 and 90 Shore A, no back-up ring, capped at 0.40 mm. Fitted to the shape of the classic extrusion diagrams as an early check — always verify against the supplier's own diagram, which also covers cross section and temperature.

Assessment limits are common design recommendations, not requirements from a standard, and supplier limits may differ. Not modelled: compression set over time, friction, eccentricity, ovality, surface roughness, gas permeation, housing deflection under pressure, spiral failure, rotary motion. The calculation is geometric and is not a conformity check against ISO 3601-2.
